We often ask whether young people have purpose. We rarely ask whether the conditions around youth support it.

This asymmetry can contribute to deficit-thinking about what youth are missing within themselves, while letting environments, institutions, and relationships that shape their development off the hook. This exchange is about changing how we see youth capacities for purposeful experience.

About & Context

This is a place to learn in public

Portraits of Purposeful Youth is an open exchange hosted by the Purpose and Identity Processes (PIP) Lab at Cornell University. PIP Lab is an academic research lab interested in how young people develop purpose and identity in real contexts.

The goal of this exchange is not to turn visitors into research participants, but to help improve the quality of our questions. We want our research to stay grounded in the issues, tensions, and solutions that matter to young people and the systems around them.

How to engage with this exchange

Information shared here is meant to guide inquiry through interaction. Responses are not treated as survey data, counted as findings, or used as a substitute for ethical research design.

What people share helps us notice where the framework is clear, where it misses lived experience, and what questions future research might ask more usefully and responsibly.

  • 1
    Ground the work. Keep our questions connected to real settings, not just academic silos.
  • 2
    Surface better questions. Help us see what actually needs inquiry before we design studies around it.
  • 3
    Respect different kinds of knowing. Treat practice, lived experience, and scholarship as distinguishable and equally beneficial sources of guidance.
The Claim
A sense of purpose is not distributed evenly across young people. It is cultivated or constrained by the unique set of conditions that surround them.

This is a claim with evidence behind it. Purpose — a self-organizing and forward-looking aim — is a developmental resource. But it does not emerge by chance. It grows in relation to what young people can see, what they are given the chance to do, who recognizes them, and what their environment makes possible.

What your perspective informs

  • What researchers study — shifting from traits and scores to mechanisms and conditions
  • What practitioners build — designing environments that actively cultivate purpose
  • What policymakers fund — holding institutions accountable for creating conditions that support purposeful development
  • Who is seen as purposeful — recognizing that constrained purpose often reflects constrained conditions, not constrained potential
What makes this an exchange?
If purpose develops in conditions,
then knowledge about purpose development
must recognize and engage with those conditions.

A framework that engages only with academic theory is limited in what it can accomplish. It treats knowledge as fixed, delivered from above, and available to too few people. More generative knowledge has to be cultivated through practice, recognition, and conditions that invite many ways of knowing. The academic conversation around purpose has been happening in a closed loop. This is an opportunity to open it.

"The promise of this exchange is realized when the framework that emerges is shaped by the people, places, and conditions it is trying to understand."
The Framework

Introducing the Portraits of Purposeful Youth Framework:
Eight Lenses of Purposeful Experience

The eight lenses are meant to connect academic perspectives on purpose development with the lived experience of young people and the people who know, teach, support, and work alongside them. These eight lenses are a starting place for expanding the collective vocabulary for seeing purpose development. Much of the work on purpose has emphasized what one's purpose is, or how strongly purpose is felt. Those questions matter, but they may not be enough. The lenses help us notice additional conditions, processes, and forms of support that shape purpose development. Use them to tell us what feels useful, what feels off, and where the language still fails to capture what happens in real settings.

01 · Inner Experience
Research-grounded
Sense of Purpose
How purposeful one feels. The subjective experience of perceiving that one's actions carry meaning and direction — the felt sense of purpose, distinct from being able to articulate or explain it.
+ What to look for in practice
In practiceDoes this young person report that what they do matters? Do they express a sense of drive or direction — even without words for it? Is there something they return to, even when it's hard?
→ See exchange discussions
02 · Inner Experience
Actively developing
Scope and Awareness
What a young person can imagine, see, or name as possible targets for purpose. The horizon of possibility — the range of lives, roles, and forms of contribution they can conceive of for someone like themselves.
+ What to look for in practice
In practiceCan this young person name roles, causes, or futures they care about? Has their horizon expanded over time, or stayed fixed? Constrained scope often reflects constrained exposure — not constrained capacity.
→ See exchange discussions
03 · Inner Experience
Research-grounded
Orientation
The broad domain or general direction that frames a young person's purpose — whether prosocial, financial, vocational, religious, civic, creative, or otherwise. Orientation names the general category of contribution or becoming that organizes how they see themselves in relation to the world.
+ What to look for in practice
In practiceDoes this young person have a general sense of what kind of contributor or person they want to become — even without a specific aim yet? Is their orientation toward serving others, building something, achieving mastery, or following a calling? Orientation can be present before specific goals form.
→ See exchange discussions
04 · Inner Experience
Actively developing
Content
The specific aspirational substance of what a young person understands they want to work toward. Not just a broad orientation, but a particular aim: being a great student, helping advance an important issue, mastering a craft, building something that matters. The articulation — even if partial or evolving — of what their purposeful effort is actually directed at.
+ What to look for in practice
In practiceCan this young person name something specific they want to do, become, or contribute — not just a general domain? Has that aspiration grown more concrete over time? Even a vague but genuine aim ("I want to do something with the environment") is purposeful content worth developing.
→ See exchange discussions
05 · Active Engagement
Actively developing
Opportunities for Practice
Concrete chances to explore, try, and enact purposeful action. Not just exposure — genuine opportunities to act, contribute, and experience oneself as a capable agent in something that matters beyond the self.
+ What to look for in practice
In practiceDoes this young person have regular, authentic chances to act on what they care about? Are those opportunities real — with stakes and impact — or performative? Who controls access to them?
→ See exchange discussions
06 · Active Engagement
Research-grounded
Recognition and Reflection
Feedback, validation, and structured sense-making from oneself and others. The relational and reflective processes through which purposeful experience becomes visible, interpreted, and integrated into identity.
+ What to look for in practice
In practiceDo adults and peers name and affirm what this young person contributes? Are there structured opportunities to reflect and extract meaning? Is this young person developing a narrative of their own purposeful identity?
→ See exchange discussions
07 · Conditions
Actively developing
Environmental Affordances
Material, social, and institutional resources that make purposeful action possible. The structures, spaces, and relationships that either enable or foreclose what a young person can do with what they care about.
+ What to look for in practice
In practiceWhat resources does this young person have access to? Who gatekeeps them? Are the environments this young person inhabits designed with purposeful development in mind — or does it happen despite the design?
→ See exchange discussions
08 · Conditions
Empirically emerging
Cultural Context
The sociocultural signals — explicit and implicit — about what kinds of purpose are valued, legitimate, and possible in a given context. The messages a young person receives about who gets to pursue purpose, and what kinds count.
+ What to look for in practice
In practiceWhat does this environment communicate about whose futures matter and which forms of contribution are worth pursuing? Are cultural norms expanding or narrowing what this young person believes is possible for someone like them?
→ See exchange discussions
Features that guide how purpose functions

Put the Features to Work

By features, we mean academic concepts that can be applied to purpose as opportunities for study, investigation, and real-world evaluation. They help us ask not only whether purpose is present, but how purpose becomes more or less visible in particular settings. These features are not unique to purpose. They are attributes that might shape what we see through the various lenses of purpose, informing how purpose becomes visible, durable, meaningful, and supported across settings. We think they may be functional ingredients that bring the lenses to light, but that remains an open area of inquiry. If you were looking at one young person, program, classroom, or community, which feature would matter most first, and what would that priority help us ask?

Your working order

Move a feature up or down based on what feels most consequential in the setting you have in mind. There is no correct order; the point is to make your reasoning visible.

For researchers and scholars
The framework carries an open epistemic status — different lenses have different evidentiary foundations, stated explicitly. What we need is honest engagement with those differences: where you see convergence with existing literature, where you see tension, what empirical approaches you'd propose, and what the open questions board is missing. The exchange is designed for sustained dialogue, not just single posts.
Start a research discussion
For practitioners and educators
Usability is tested in practice, not in theory. We need honest description: what these lenses look like in your setting, which ones feel accurate and which feel off, where the framework gives you useful language and where it lets you down. The most valuable contributions are specific — a particular program, student, or moment where something clicked or didn't. Bring those. The Prompt Bank is also yours: if you've found a question that opens things up, share it.

For policymakers and funders
The framework is designed to be actionable at the systems level. What we need is pressure-testing: Does this framework give you useful language for accountability decisions? Does it surface the right questions for investment? Where does it fall short of what you need to actually change institutional practice? The goal is a framework that can inform policy — but that requires the policy community to tell us where it needs to go further.

For young people
This framework was built by researchers trying to describe something you live. That means it's probably right about some things and wrong or incomplete about others — and only you can tell us which is which.
